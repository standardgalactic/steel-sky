# The Department of Unclaimed Rooms: Treatment

Catalogue slot 01.01 | Cycle: Cities That Think | Framework: map versus territory, classification | Kind M/C | Scale M

## Logline

Thirty days before a refit of a six-storey building begins, a municipal surveyor measuring it for the final register finds five occupied rooms that appear on no current floor plan, each held by a resident with solid paper proof of living there. She restores the rooms to the map, certain that an accurate map will repair the mismatch. It repairs nothing. Each correction decides who is counted, who is rehoused, and who is quietly left outside the city's sight, and she has to learn that the register was never a copy of the building but a rule for what counts.

## Framework and conditions

The story dramatizes classification. A register is a function from physical things to categories, and every category carries rights and duties. Putting a room on the map does not copy it; it applies a rule to it.

- **The territory is not mysterious.** Every room in the building can be measured with a tape. The story does not claim that reality is unknowable. What is contested is which rooms count as dwellings, and what follows when they do.
- **Visibility depends on the scheme, not on presence.** A room that is invisible to one office is listed by another because the offices use different inclusion rules.
- **Every correction has a selection effect.** Adding to a map under a fixed budget or capacity removes something else. The fiction states each correction and who it adds and removes.
- **No register is wrong by its own rule.** The error is believing that one register ought to equal the territory.

## Setting and deadline

Calloway House, a six-storey former hotel converted in 1974 into 48 flats, is to begin a structural refit in thirty days. The refit's rehousing plan is generated from the Department of Building Registers' plan of the building. Three recurring locations carry the film:

- **The building.** A central stair, narrow corridors, a rear service strip with a light well, and doors that the plan does not draw.
- **The Register Room.** A basement office in the Department of Building Registers, flat files, plan chests and a terminal that validates every entry against the Housing Standard.
- **The caretaker's room.** Ground floor, behind the lobby: keys on boards, a ledger, a kettle, a hatch onto the street.

A thirty-day countdown runs through the book. Day 30 is the survey. Day 0 is the day the contractor takes the north stack.

## Principal characters

**Ilse Varga, 41, surveyor.** Department of Building Registers. Trusts drawn dimensions and believes an accurate drawing is a repairable one. Precise, tired, conscientious. Has never knowingly altered a record.

**Desmond Okafor, 59, Registrar.** Her superior and keeper of the classification manual. Not an obstructionist. He wants the refit to start, the grant to be spent, and the register to stay defensible. Thinks of the plan as the instrument by which the city sees.

**Ottilie Brandt, 84, resident of Room 3R.** Has lived in her room since 1982. Her papers are in a shoebox, mostly in her late husband's name. She will not sign anything that describes her room as unclassified.

**Teodor Kask, 35, courier, resident of Room 5R.** Has six independent records of his address, from the bank, library, electoral roll, a mobile account and others. Practical, wary of forms, good at paper.

**Gordon Pike, 67, caretaker.** In the building since 1979. Keeps a key ledger and a drawer of handwritten tenancy slips. Believes he did people a favour and committed an offence in the same act.

Absent figure: **E. Lindqvist**, the 1974 conversion clerk whose rule excluded the rear rooms.

## Ground truth (private development ledger)

1. In 1931 the building is a hotel with 48 guest suites and 5 small rear staff rooms, each on its own service landing (Rooms 2R to 6R, one per floor from the second floor up). Each staff room has its own electricity sub-meter.
2. In 1974 the hotel is converted into 48 flats. The conversion drawing covers only the front of the building. The rear strip is left blank, outside the scope of the works. The 1974 dwelling standard requires at least 9 square metres, a window onto a street or a court at least 3 metres wide, and a door onto a shared corridor. The rear rooms measure between 7.4 and 8.6 square metres and open onto a light well 2.4 metres wide.
3. The department's working plan is the 1974 drawing, so the rear strip does not exist on it. The 1931 hotel plans are in the heritage archive, held by a different office, and show the rooms as staff rooms.
4. Gordon Pike becomes caretaker in 1979. He starts letting the dry, furnished rear rooms to people the parish sends him. He writes a tenancy slip for each and collects rent into a cash tin. The rooms remain physically unchanged.
5. The occupants register with other offices. Electricity bills them as the occupants of the sub-meters. The electoral roll lists "Rm 3R, Calloway House". The library, a bank and a mobile account accept the room as an address. The department's register never learns of any of it, because it asks only whether a space is drawn on the plan and meets the standard.
6. Three listed flats are empty, and have been for years. One tenant died in 2021, one left in 2022 and one in 2023. Their tenancy records were never closed because closing needs a form that nobody filed. The register still counts them as occupied.
7. The refit's rehousing plan for phase one (the north stack, which includes rear rooms 4R, 5R and 6R) has 10 places, set from the register's count of 8 listed households in that stack. It was never run against the real building.

## Register visibility (the central exhibit)
